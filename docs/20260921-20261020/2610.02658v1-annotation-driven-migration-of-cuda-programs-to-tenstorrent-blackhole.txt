Abstract —Tenstorrent Blackhole combines distributed local memories, explicit inter-core communication, and Tensix cores decoupling data movement from computation. CUDA offers a substantial HPC software base but leaves physical data placement and scheduling largely implicit. Migrating CUDA HPC kernels to Blackhole requires spatial mapping across cores and per-core coordination of compute and data-movement kernels. We present an MLIR-based compiler deriving data placement, inter-core communication, and tile computation from a statically shaped affine CUDA subset. Declarative annotations express choices not fixed by the source, including compute/DM operation placement and streaming granularity. We evaluate it on Gaussian elimination, five-point stencil, and symmetric rank-k update. Relative to our compiler’s default realizations, the best measured configurations achieve a 4.2× speedup for BF16 Gaussian and 

2.1–2.2× for FP32 Gaussian and Jacobi. A choice’s performance impact can reverse with surrounding policies, precision, and loop schedule, motivating comparison of alternative per-core realizations rather than independent policy selection. 

Index Terms —CUDA, High performance computing, Manycore processors, MLIR, Pipeline processing, Tenstorrent 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Spatial accelerators have emerged as hardware platforms for data-intensive computation, combining distributed local memories with explicit on-chip communication [1]. Cerebras wafer-scale engines, AMD Versal AI Engines, and Tenstorrent Tensix core arrays are prominent examples [2]–[4]. Beyond AI, such systems have also been explored for HPC workloads, including stencils and FFTs [5]–[11]. Tenstorrent Blackhole presents an additional mapping challenge: each Tensix core de-couples data movement from computation [4]. Separate RISC-V processors execute compute and data-movement (DM) ker-nels and communicate through circular buffers (CBs) in L1 memory. Mapping data and computation across Tensix cores is therefore only part of the problem: the compiler must also divide operations between the compute and DM kernels and coordinate their execution within each core. We refer to this intra-core mapping problem as per-core realization , a central focus of this work. Tenstorrent’s standard TTIR-to-TTNN path represents com-putation through tensor-level operations but does not expose the low-level data movement and synchronization choices used in optimized HPC kernels. Prior Tenstorrent HPC studies [8]– [11] optimize kernels such as stencils and FFTs in TT-Metalium, rather than provide a general compilation path for such programs. At a higher level, TileLoom [12] automates spatiotemporal mapping and inter-core dataflow for workloads such as FlashAttention and GEMM written in tile-based languages. These efforts leave open a general compilation path that exposes alternative per-core realizations for HPC workloads with varied affine access and computation patterns. A substantial body of HPC kernels and benchmark suites is available in CUDA [13], [14]. CUDA’s SIMT abstraction de-scribes computation and memory accesses over threads while leaving physical data placement, movement, and scheduling implicit. Migrating these programs to Blackhole will improve the programming experience, but requires deriving both spatial tile dataflow and a decoupled per-core realization. We present an MLIR-based compiler that performs this migration for an affine subset of CUDA whose per-core working sets fit in local L1. Given affine iteration domains and access maps together with a compact placement specification, the compiler assigns data and computation to the core grid and derives the communication required for each access. Within each core, it lowers thread-level computation to tile operations, constructs their operands using within-tile transformations, and converts guards to tile masks. It then partitions the operations between the compute and DM kernels and coordinates their execution using L1 buffers and CB synchronization. The key technical challenge in this migration is that source semantics do not determine a unique per-core realization. For example, arithmetic may run in a compute or DM kernel; a guard may become a compute-side mask or a DM-side copy; work may be divided between the two DM kernels; and CB synchronization may operate at different granularities. The performance of each choice depends on the resulting balance of compute and DM work, so assigning a fixed cost to each choice can be insufficient. We expose these choices as declarative annotations. The compiler checks their applicability, applies the corresponding IR transformations, and lowers the result without handwritten low-level kernels. We evaluate Gaussian elimination, five-point stencil, and symmetric rank-k update using CUDA programs derived from benchmark suites, subject to the scope and target-independent canonicalization of Section II-C; for each workload, all con-figurations share the same CUDA input and differ only in their annotation sets. A. Tenstorrent Blackhole Architecture 

Blackhole is a spatial architecture that integrates 140 Tensix cores on a chip, of which our evaluated device exposes a 13 × 10 logical worker grid to TT-Metalium. Each core has 1.5 MiB of L1 SRAM. Figure 1 shows the execution organization of a Tensix core. Two RISC-V DM processors issue transfers to DRAM and remote cores through the two NoC interfaces [4]. In TT-Metalium, DM and compute kernels communicate through CBs in L1 memory. The CBs stage operands and results and provide producer–consumer synchro-nization, allowing data transfer and computation to overlap. We refer to DM0, DM1, and the compute pipeline as engines .Computation on a Tensix core is organized over 32 × 32 -element tiles, each arranged as four 16 × 16 -element faces. The unpacker loads tiles from input CBs into source registers, the FPU computes from these registers into Dst registers, and the packer writes Dst tiles to an output CB. The SFPU instead performs operations on values already staged in Dst. For BF16 binary operations, the FPU is used when at least one operand is unpacked from CBs and the SFPU when both already reside in Dst. FP32 element-wise operations use the SFPU. DRAM

B. Compiler Infrastructure 

We use Polygeist [15] to translate CUDA programs into MLIR [16], enabling its affine-raising pass so that eligi-ble loops, conditions, and array accesses are represented in MLIR’s Affine Dialect. Our compiler ultimately emits the D2M 

(Direct-to-Metal) Dialect of tt-mlir rather than the standard TTIR-to-TTNN path, because D2M can express computation over sharded tensors and core grids together with explicit engine assignment, data movement, and synchronization. 

C. Target Workloads and Scope 

We evaluate the compiler on three CUDA workloads: 

gaussian from Rodinia [13] and jacobi_2d and syrk 

from PolyBench/ACC [14]. gaussian performs the guarded elimination update Aij ← Aij − Ait /A tt · Atj over i > t and 

j ≥ t. Mapping this update requires inter-core transfers and within-tile broadcasts to align the pivot-dependent operands while preserving the update domain. jacobi_2d repeatedly applies a five-point stencil to interior points, leaving the global array boundary unchanged. The stencil operands are constructed using within-tile shifts and tile-boundary fills from adjacent tiles, with inter-core transfers when those tiles reside on neighboring cores. syrk computes C ← αAA T + βC .Its reduction is realized as tile matrix multiplications with accumulated partial results and can be blocked to expose additional reuse. We use the jacobi_2d kernel body without restructuring, but manually adapt the other two inputs to limitations of the current frontend. For gaussian , we omit the update of the right-hand-side vector b because our current frontend does not support distributing rank-1 arrays. For syrk , we rewrite PolyBench’s in-place reduction using a scalar accumulator followed by a single final update, Cij = α acc + βC ij . The current frontend requires this target-independent canonicaliza-tion, which can change floating-point rounding. For all three workloads, we set the CUDA thread-block size to 32 × 32 ,because the current lowering maps the threads of one block to the elements of one Tensix tile. These adaptations add no workload-specific logic to the lowering and can be eliminated through future extensions (Section IV-F). All three are eval-uated at problem sizes whose per-core working sets remain within each core’s 1.5 MiB L1, so that the measurements are not dominated by DRAM bandwidth. 

D. Prior Work on HPC Acceleration for Tenstorrent 

Prior Tenstorrent stencil studies expose different data-movement bottlenecks depending on where data is kept between iterations. Brown and Barton [8] stream a 2-D 

jacobi_2d stencil through device DRAM on Grayskull. Their optimizations favor fewer, larger, contiguous DRAM requests and reduce redundant local data copying. Piarulli and De Sensi [9] instead use heterogeneous CPU–Wormhole im-plementations of a 2-D five-point stencil. The CPU constructs shifted submatrices or a stencil-to-row layout, and data crosses PCIe on every iteration, making CPU-side preprocessing and transfer overhead central to their analysis. Taylor et al. [10] keep intermediate values in per-core L1 rather than DRAM and implement a 3-D seven-point stencil with halo exchange on Wormhole. Neighboring elements are aligned through tile shifts; east–west shifts require a transpose, shift, and transpose back, while tile transposition enables halo columns to be transferred as contiguous row segments. They find that local data rearrangement and computation collectively cost more than halo communication. We therefore treat within-tile transformations and arithmetic as optimization targets in the per-core realization design space. III. M APPING SIMT TO A DECOUPLED ACCELERATOR 

A. From CUDA Constructs to Blackhole 

Tenstorrent’s distributed L1 memories allow a global array to be sharded across cores so that each core owns part of the array, which remains in what we call persistent L1 for the entire execution. We target workloads whose per-core working sets fit in local L1. Larger working sets additionally require scheduling DRAM transfers and managing tile reuse in L1; such planning is outside our current scope and complementary to work such as TileLoom [12]. Within our scope, we follow the owner-computes rule: each output region is computed by the core that owns it. Affine accesses to data owned by other cores identify inter-core communication, which the compiler realizes as NoC transfers, using multicast where possible. Once the operands have been delivered, thread-level com-putation must be converted into per-core tile computation. The current mapping associates the two CUDA thread-index dimensions with the row and column positions of a 32 ×32 tile. A more general mapping could first express CUDA thread and block dimensions as loops and then select two loop dimensions as the tile axes, as explored by T2T [17]. The compiler then recognizes reductions and replaces them with tile-level reduction or matrix operations. Guards restrict the active ele-ments through masks, while non-identical access maps require within-tile shifts, broadcasts, transposes, or boundary patches to align each operand with its consumer. Finally, the default realization partitions the per-core pro-gram by assigning arithmetic to the compute kernel and data manipulation to the DM kernels. The compiler then inserts L1 buffers between producers and consumers and schedules their synchronization. GPUs can hide data-movement latency through warp scheduling, whereas on Blackhole the buffering and synchronization for overlap must be represented explicitly. 

B. Per-Core Realization Design Space 

A DM kernel is not limited to moving data: with access to L1 and a NoC, it can also transform and compute on data through RISC-V-based scalar processing and NoC-assisted bulk copies. The same operation can therefore be realized on DM in this way, or on compute as a tile operation. Table I summarizes the policy dimensions exposed by the compiler. A guard can be realized as a mask tile and a tile-wise select in the compute kernel, or as a DM-side copy that overwrites only the selected elements. A single row or column used as a tile operand can be replicated across the other tile dimension by a DM kernel, or kept as a line and expanded during compute-side consumption. Element-wise arithmetic on such a row or column need not be performed as a full-tile operation; a DM kernel can instead apply it element by element while producing the line.

The physical tile layout creates a further choice. Row-wise shifts and copies access 16-byte-aligned contiguous segments and can use bulk NoC transfers. Direct column-wise operations access strided elements and require repeated scalar loads and stores by a DM kernel. Alternatively, a column-wise operation can be realized by transposing the tile and applying the corresponding row-wise operation, adding transposition overhead while enabling bulk transfers. Streaming granularity and CB depth jointly shape the trade-off between pipeline overlap and L1 usage. Finer granularity may expose more overlap, but it also increases synchronization frequency and can fragment bulk transfers. These policy choices can generally be specified indepen-dently by value and application site, while their performance effects can interact. We expose them as declarative annota-tions, allowing the compiler to lower different combinations without handwritten low-level kernels. IV. C OMPILER DESIGN

2) Automatic Lowering: The current lowering maps the two CUDA thread-index dimensions to the row and column positions of a 32 × 32 tile. To preserve the owner-computes rule when a CUDA kernel offsets its output indices, the compiler rebases affine accesses and guards to the destination coordinates. For gaussian , this converts Rodinia’s indices, which write A[xidx + t + 1 , y idx + t], into an output-coordinate iteration space. It then derives per-core shard loops from the tensor shapes and selected core grid. Affine loads become logical tile reads that retain their element access maps, scalar arithmetic becomes tile operations, and stores become tile writes. Affine conditions are represented as tile-region predi-cates, while sequential and reduction loops become temporal tile loops. Host-side loops around CUDA kernel launches are lifted into device-side temporal loops, with cross-core dependences enforced later through barriers. 3) Derived Optimizations: After applying C2 annotations, the compiler derives communication and reuse optimizations. A transfer whose region is partly local is split into a local read and a remote delivery, and a rectangular delivery region is requested as one frame. The compiler also identifies frame deliveries that can use multicast across a grid row or column. For loop-level reuse, A[t, j ] in gaussian varies with the inner-loop index j but not the outer-loop index i. The compiler prepares a frame of the required values across j before the 

i loop and reuses it across that loop’s iterations. Transpose simplification combines T (a)+ T (b) into T (a+b) and cancels adjacent transpose pairs.

The compiler validates lane assignments to prevent cross-lane sharing of synchronized buffers and conflicting accesses to persistent L1 buffers. In jacobi_2d , the construction of the north and south operands can be assigned to one lane and that of the west and east operands to the other. The split is legal because the north and south construction reads the original view of A, while the west and east construction reads the separate converted view %A_t .

E. Physical — Resource Planning and D2M Emission 

Physicalization resolves the logical resources introduced at C3 and emits D2M IR. It realizes the synchronization with CB reserve/push and wait/pop operations, assigning each synchronized buffer depth one by default and depth two when streamed; a non-streamed delivery uses depth two only when explicitly annotated and it passes the compiler’s legality checks. From each buffer’s shape and depth, it derives the CB capacity and allocates the L1 storage. For %west_operand 

in Listing 5, the compiler allocates L1 storage and CB capacity for one shard row per CB slot with row streaming, or one tile per slot with column streaming. Cross-core RAW and WAR dependences on persistent L1 operands are enforced with grid-wide semaphore barriers placed before remote reads and before subsequent updates by the owner core, respectively. For Dst allocation, the compiler places a scope inside the in-nermost loop that writes an output tile, assigns slots to live val-ues by linear scan, and spills values crossing scope boundaries to L1. A Dst-resident matmul accumulator stays within one bank across the reduction loop, whereas an L1-resident one is emitted as a linalg.generic whose output panel the downstream D2M pipeline divides into Dst-sized subblocks. Each Task phase is finally emitted as d2m.generic over its core grid, with its DM0, DM1, and compute groups as concurrent threads. Host–DRAM transfers, layout conversion, and DRAM–persistent-L1 movement are emitted as separate dispatches. 

F. Scope 

The current implementation targets affine kernels over statically shaped rank-2 tensors with positive extents. Each CUDA thread block must match the 32 × 32 tile shape. All tensor extents must be multiples of that tile shape, and each output tile grid must divide evenly across the core grid because tail-domain schedules are not generated. The current compiler does not support CUDA __shared__ memory or __syncthreads() . Cross-core transfers cannot yet be split into streamed remote loads because their RAW and WAR barriers synchronize the grid around remote reads and subsequent owner updates of an entire persistent L1 buffer. E. Numerical Accuracy 

We report relative Frobenius error for the fastest configura-tion at each precision, against an FP64 reference computed in PyTorch from the same inputs. For gaussian on a diagonally dominant input, the FP32 error is 9.3 × 10 −7 over the off-diagonal; for jacobi_2d , it is 9.5 × 10 −6 over the interior after 1000 steps. At BF16, the corresponding errors increase to 4.7×10 −3 for 

gaussian and 1.68 for jacobi_2d . Both device outputs are nevertheless reproduced bit-exactly, at their full evaluated depths, by a simulator that models the emitted operation order, the FPU/SFPU assignment, the BF16 constants, and the accumulation of multiplier partial products in BF16 Dst with fp32_dest_acc_en=false . The increase therefore reflects BF16 representation and Tensix arithmetic; in partic-ular, 0.2 is not exactly representable in BF16, introducing a systematic bias at every jacobi_2d step. For syrk at BF16, the whole-output error is 1.0 × 10 −2; a simulator of the measured matrix-unit arithmetic likewise reproduces the device output bit-exactly. Future annotations could selectively promote operation and Dst-accumulation precision, trading accuracy against Dst capacity and execution time. 

F. Toward Feedback-Driven Annotation Selection 

The annotation space is large because policy choices can be combined and, where applicable, specified independently for different axes. Even without varying CB depth, the evaluated BF16 space contains 336 configurations for gaussian and 256 for jacobi_2d , all of which executed successfully. These conservative counts exclude unlikely but legal choices, such as sinking masks along unrelated axes or adding more compute–DM handoffs. The performance differences in Ta-bles II–IV and the policy interactions in Fig. 4 motivate exposing and comparing alternative per-core realizations rather than relying on fixed per-annotation costs. We therefore envision a feedback-driven system in which an LLM proposes annotation sets and uses measured performance to guide subsequent proposals, while the compiler applies each set and completes the lowering. Structural legality checking and low-level code generation remain within the compiler, while the LLM searches the declarative annotation space. VI. R ELATED WORK 

Prior work retargets GPU programs across architectures. MKPipe [18] pipelines OpenCL multi-kernel workloads on FPGAs and balances throughput and resources across kernels, while Moses et al. [19] translate CUDA parallel constructs to CPUs. Booth [20] accepts CUDA, HIP, and Triton and emits Tenstorrent Metalium through a Tile DataFlow IR that represents regions, communication channels, transfer and synchronization actions, and L1 placement. HetGPU [21] instead proposes a compiler and runtime layer for cross-vendor binary compatibility by mapping CUDA SIMT semantics to heterogeneous GPUs, including Tenstorrent’s many-core MIMD execution model. Our work instead focuses on affine CUDA HPC kernels and preserves flexibility in their per-core realization through declarative annotations. T2T [17] and TileLoom [12] address complementary thread-to-tile mapping and spatiotemporal dataflow planning, respectively, as dis-cussed in Section III. Accelerator-oriented programming systems instead expose spatial execution and data movement directly to the pro-grammer. TT-Lang [22] provides a Python DSL for writing Tenstorrent kernels that progressively discloses pipelining and synchronization details. Dato [23] similarly provides a task-based model for FPGA and NPU dataflow accelerators, in which tasks are connected through explicit streams and input sharding is expressed as layout types. In contrast, our compiler starts from existing CUDA code rather than a new DSL, and derives inter-core communication from its affine accesses rather than requiring explicit tasks and streams as in Dato. ARIES [24] and Tiramisu [25] provide user-directed sched-ules for choices such as computation placement and loop transformations. Our annotations instead target fine-grained per-core realization choices for decoupled compute and data-movement processors, including operation placement, guard realization, and streaming granularity. TT-Lang is also de-signed to support AI-assisted translation and optimization in which AI agents generate and refine programs in its DSL, whereas Merouani et al. [26] use an LLM to propose Tiramisu transformation sequences whose legality and performance are evaluated by the compiler. Our checkpoints could similarly support LLM-proposed annotations, with the compiler check-ing legality and performing low-level lowering. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented an MLIR-based compiler that derives spa-tial mapping and per-core realization when migrating affine CUDA programs to Tenstorrent Blackhole, exposing per-core realizations as declarative annotations and lowering their com-binations automatically. The fastest measured per-core config-urations improve performance by 4.2 × on BF16 gaussian 

and 2.1–2.2 × on FP32 gaussian and jacobi_2d , relative to the compiler’s own default realizations. The measurements also show that the effect of an annotation depends on the surrounding policies. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
