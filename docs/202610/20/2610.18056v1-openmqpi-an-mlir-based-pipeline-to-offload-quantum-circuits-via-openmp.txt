## Abstract 

The emerging field of quantum computing requires specialized software stacks and programming interfaces. However, these tools are often tightly coupled to specific software ecosystems and hard-ware backends, limiting interoperability and software reuse. For High-Performance Computing-Quantum Computing (HPCQC) ap-plication developers, this complicates the integration of quantum accelerators into existing applications. Existing quantum programming frameworks expose circuit rep-resentations and compilation pipelines that are tightly coupled to their execution ecosystems, making it difficult to integrate quantum compilation with established heterogeneous compiler infrastruc-tures. To address this issue, we propose OpenMQPI, a Multi-Level Intermediate Representation (MLIR)-based OpenMP extension for quantum programming. OpenMQPI leverages OpenMP’s standard-ized target-offloading infrastructure to provide a reusable approach for integrating quantum resources into High Performance Comput-ing (HPC) applications. To enable quantum offloading with Open-MQPI, we create QPIR, a lightweight MLIR dialect for representing quantum circuits inside the OpenMP target regions, together with a compiler pipeline that performs quantum-specific optimizations and lowers the resulting representation into an executable compat-ible with the OpenMP offloading model. At runtime, the generated executable leverages the OpenMP target offloading model via a MQSS plugin within OpenMQPI to offload quantum circuits to the quantum hardware. We evaluate OpenMQPI using the Variational Quantum Eigen-solver (VQE), the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT), and the Deutsch– Jozsa algorithm with 5, 10, 15, and 20 qubits, and compare its run-time performance against Qiskit and CUDA Quantum. The results show that OpenMQPI achieves the lowest average execution time for VQE across all evaluated qubit configurations. At 20 qubits, OpenMQPI requires 5.18 s, compared with 5.83 s for Qiskit and 7.39 s for CUDA Quantum. For QFT and Deutsch–Jozsa, OpenMQPI remains close to Qiskit while achieving lower execution times than CUDA Quantum.

## 1 Introduction 

Quantum computing has the potential to address computational problems that are believed to be unsolvable for classical comput-ers [37]. In practice, however, many quantum applications also rely on classical computation to support and coordinate quantum execution [8, 10, 38]. This has motivated growing interest in in-tegrating quantum computing resources into HPC environments, where classical and quantum resources can work together within hybrid applications [3]. Conspicuous progress has been made in the development of quantum computing hardware [29, 31]. However, to achieve the full potential of Quantum Computing (QC), this hardware requires compatible programming languages, interfaces, and compilation infrastructure that can coordinate classical and quantum resources within a common execution environment. A variety of programming interfaces, including Qiskit[13], PennyLane[4], and CUDA Quantum[18], provide tools for developing and exe-cuting quantum applications. However, these frameworks rely on different programming models, compiler infrastructures, and execu-tion environments. As a result, integrating quantum resources into existing HPC applications often requires developers to work with framework-specific software stacks and interfaces. This framework-specific approach limits the portability and reusability of software components across different HPCQC environments. A key challenge is that traditional HPC offloading and quan-tum execution expose different compilation and execution models. OpenMP target offloading is designed around launching compiled device code on a target device, whereas a quantum resource ex-ecutes a circuit or quantum job rather than conventional device instructions. Therefore, the quantum circuit must remain available as an ex-plicit representation beyond the device-code lowering stage. Di-rectly treating a quantum resource as another OpenMP target device does not provide a natural mechanism to preserve and optimize the circuit while still using the OpenMP compilation and runtime infrastructure. This observation motivates a different approach: treating quan-tum circuits as first-class target payloads rather than as machine code. OpenMQPI preserves the circuit at the compiler level, applies arXiv:2610.18056v1 [cs.PL] 13 Oct 2026 Ercüment Kaya, Ekin Devim Şahinkaya, Minh Chung, and Martin Schulz 

quantum-specific transformations in MLIR, and then transports the resulting circuit through the OpenMP target-offloading path to a quantum-specific runtime plugin. In this paper, we introduce OpenMQPI, an MLIR-based compila-tion infrastructure that integrates quantum circuit compilation and execution into the OpenMP target-offloading model. Rather than introducing another framework-specific programming interface, OpenMQPI builds on the existing OpenMP infrastructure, allowing quantum resources to be integrated as target devices within the programming model already widely used in HPC. OpenMQPI preserves the quantum circuit as an Intermediate Representation (IR) during the compilation rather than lowering it to target device instructions. The MLIR-based representation enables quantum-specific optimizations before the circuits are low-ered for execution. The resulting circuit is then carried through the OpenMP offloading pipeline and delivered to a quantum-specific 

libomptarget plugin, which submits the circuit to the selected quantum resource. The implementation leverages the OpenMP runtime and MQSS Client interface [15] to submit quantum circuits to the selected quan-tum device. The approach is considered a custom libomptarget 

plugin, referred to as the MQSS plugin. We evaluate OpenMQPI using the VQE, the QFT, and the Deutsch–Jozsa algorithm across different qubit configurations. Our evaluation validates the com-plete compilation-and-execution pipeline and demonstrates that OpenMQPI maintains competitive runtime performance compared with existing quantum programming approaches. This paper makes the following contributions: 

• An MLIR-based representation for quantum circuits: 

We introduce Quantum Programming Intermediate Repre-sentation (QPIR), a lightweight MLIR dialect for represent-ing and optimizing quantum circuits. 

• Circuit-preserving integration with OpenMP target offloading : We extend the OpenMP target-offloading in-frastructure to represent quantum circuits as target pay-loads, preserve them through compilation, and deliver them to the MQSS plugin for execution. 

• A compiler and runtime pipeline for quantum ex-ecution: We implement qpir_cc, qpir_opt, and qpir_link together with an MQSS plugin to generate, optimize, lower, and execute quantum circuits through the OpenMP offload-ing infrastructure. 

• An experimental evaluation of OpenMQPI: We evalu-ate the proposed infrastructure and its runtime performance across different quantum applications. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 covers background. In Section 3, we introduce the OpenMQPI, dis-cuss its architecture and components. In Section 4, we present the experiments and performance evaluation. Section 5 reviews the related literature. Finally, we conclude the paper and discuss future work in Section 6. 

## 2 Background 

This section briefly explains the basics of the concepts and tools that we exploit to keep this paper self-contained. 

## 2.1 HPC and QC 

HPC uses supercomputers and computer clusters to address com-plex computational problems with large data sets, large-scale simu-lations, and the modeling of complex physical phenomena [11, 40]. The HPC applications often use parallel processing and compu-tation offloading to hardware accelerators such as Graphics Pro-cessing Unit (GPU), Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA), and quantum computers to improve the performance of HPC applica-tions [1, 32, 39]. The many-core architecture of GPUs is highly suitable for data-parallel processing, while quantum computers use a fundamentally different computational model. Quantum comput-ers use radically different modalities that could change the com-plexity of several computational problems, from factorization to optimization [17]. However, this potential power comes with new requirements on users, HPC centers, and the developers. The requirements include, but are not limited to, programming interfaces, compilation infrastructure, and a hybrid HPCQC labora-tory [38]. Programming interfaces, such as Qiskit [13] and CUDA-Q [18], are used to describe quantum circuits in HPCQC applica-tions. A compilation infrastructure that is tailored for QC is required to optimize and ensure that the given circuit is executable on the target hardware.

As illustrated in Figure 1, LLVM follows a modular approach; therefore, high-level programming languages, such as C and For-tran, are lowered to common LLVM IR using Clang or Flang. The generated IR can then be optimized using LLVM passes before that, being lowered to machine code for the target architecture. This ap-proach enables LLVM to provide language- and target-independent optimizations, while also supporting target-specific optimizations. LLVM IR is designed to resemble machine code, enabling domain-agnostic optimizations. However, this approach loses explicit do-main information, such as matrix multiplication and hardware OpenMQPI: An MLIR-based Pipeline to Offload Quantum Circuits via OpenMP 

accelerators. To address this limitation, Lattner et al. propose MLIR [21, 22]. It provides a higher-level abstraction that preserves explicit information and progressively lowers it to LLVM IR to enable better optimizations. The MLIR infrastructure provides various, extensible dialects to distinguish domains. An MLIR dialect includes, but is not limited to, domain-specific operations and types. Hence, optimizers can work on the different parts of the code that make sense for them. 

## 2.3 OpenMP 

OpenMP [9] is an Application Programming Interface (API) for shared-memory parallel programming in C, C++, and Fortran, and is widely used in the HPC domain to parallelize the application with a set of compiler directives and runtime library routines. Thanks to this approach, developers can parallelize HPC applications without major changes to the source code. OpenMP follows the fork-join execution model, that is, an application starts with a single thread, and after reaching a parallel region, additional threads are created to execute the region in parallel. Once the execution of the paral-lelized section is completed, the threads join the original thread and continue the execution of the application. OpenMP was initially designed for parallel programming on CPUs. However, with the 4.0 specification [34], OpenMP introduces support for target offloading to enable the use of hardware accel-erators such as GPU. This model allows developers to use a single source code for different architectures while improving portability across heterogeneous computing systems [5]. The OpenMP offloading mechanism requires compiler and run-time support. While the compiler generates code for the host and the target device, OpenMP offloading runtime libraries, such as 

libomptarget in LLVM and libgomp in GCC, manage the exe-cution of regions on the target device. In LLVM, libomptarget 

provides a generic runtime for managing OpenMP offloading and communicates with device-specific implementations through a plu-gin interface. These plugins handle hardware-specific operations, such as device initialization, memory allocation, data transfers be-tween host and device, kernel execution, and synchronization. This separation allows the generic OpenMP runtime to support different accelerator architectures while delegating device-specific function-ality to the corresponding plugin. 

## 3 OpenMQPI 3.1 Motivation 

OpenMQPI is motivated by the idea of extending OpenMP’s es-tablished target-offloading model beyond classical accelerators to support quantum computing resources. This allows quantum re-sources to be integrated into an existing HPC programming and runtime infrastructure rather than requiring a separate execution model. At the compiler level, MLIR is particularly well-suited to this approach because its dialect-based design enables domain-specific representations to be introduced and optimized while remaining within a common compiler infrastructure. OpenMQPI leverages this flexibility to represent quantum operations through the QPIR dialect and progressively lower them into the OpenMP offloading pipeline. 

## 3.2 Design and Components 

OpenMQPI consists of a set of compiler and runtime components that extend the OpenMP offloading workflow for quantum circuit execution. On the compiler side, the QPIR dialect provides the intermediate representation for quantum circuits, while qpir_cc ,

qpir_opt , and qpir_link handle circuit generation, optimization, and lowering. On the runtime side, the MQSS plugin extends libomptarget 

to execute the resulting quantum circuits on quantum resources through the MQSS Client interface. 

3.2.1 QPIR Dialect. QPIR is a lightweight MLIR dialect designed to represent quantum circuits within the OpenMQPI compilation pipeline. During its design, we considered existing quantum repre-sentations, including CUDA Quantum’s [18] Quake [33] and Cata-lyst’s [12] Quantum [36] dialects. However, adopting these dialects would introduce additional dependencies on their corresponding software ecosystems. The QPIR dialect follows an imperative programming model and uses an identifier-based representation of quantum resources, where qubits are addressed by integer identifiers rather than repre-sented as first-class SSA values. Listing 2 shows an example QPIR circuit represented within an OpenMP target region. Quantum gates are represented as ex-plicit QPIR operations, such as qpir.h , qpir.rx , qpir.cx , and 

qpir.ry , while measurement and synchronization are represented by qpir.measure and qpir.barrier . Qubits are identified using integer indices, while rotation angles are represented as floating-point values. By keeping these operations within omp.target , QPIR represents the quantum circuit while preserving the OpenMP target structure required by the subsequent offloading stages. During target-region lowering, the quantum circuit is serialized into a textual representation rather than being converted into target-specific machine instructions. Runtime parameters are collected from the OpenMP mapping information and packaged together with the circuit into the data structures expected by libomptarget .The omp.target operation is then replaced with a call to __tgt_target_kernel ,enabling the circuit and its runtime parameters to follow the stan-dard OpenMP offloading path. The offload-registration stage adds the metadata required by 

libomptarget , including an offload entry, a device-image descrip-tor, and the corresponding registration functions. Since OpenMQPI transfers the quantum circuit as a runtime argument rather than executable device code, qpir_link embeds a minimal ELF image to satisfy the device-image structure expected by the OpenMP run-time. The MQSS plugin does not execute this image; the quantum circuit is provided separately through the runtime arguments. After the target region and offload metadata have been prepared, the remaining MLIR operations are lowered to the LLVM dialect, and intermediate type conversions are resolved. The resulting MLIR module is translated into LLVM IR and compiled with Clang to generate the final executable. Crucial aspect of this lowering is to preserve the quantum cir-cuit as a compiler-level payload rather than lowered into device instructions. Therefore, the OpenMP runtime provides the offload-ing mechanism, while the quantum-specific plugin provides the execution mechanism. This separation allows the existing OpenMP target-offloading infrastructure to be reused without requiring the quantum resource to execute an OpenMP-generated device binary.

## 4 Evaluation 

This evaluation addresses two questions. RQ1: End-to-end execu-tion. Can OpenMQPI successfully compile and execute quantum applications through the OpenMP-based offloading pipeline on a real quantum resource? RQ2: Runtime overhead and perfor-mance. What runtime overhead does OpenMQPI introduce com-pared with established quantum programming frameworks? To answer these questions, we first validate the complete compilation and execution pipeline on a real quantum computing environment. We then compare the runtime performance of OpenMQPI with Qiskit and CUDA Quantum across three representative quantum applications: VQE , QFT, and Deutsch–Jozsa. 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

All experiments were conducted in a Docker environment running Ubuntu 22.04 on a MacBook Pro equipped with an Apple M3 Pro processor and 18 GB of memory. OpenMQPI was built using LLVM 22.1.8, including MLIR and the OpenMP runtime infrastructure, together with MQSS Client interface. For the validation experiment, quantum circuits were executed on a 54-qubit quantum resource. For the performance experiments, we used a simulator to avoid variability due to queue waiting times for quantum resources. We compared OpenMQPI with Qiskit 1.3.3 and CUDA Quantum 0.15.0. All three implementations submitted their quantum circuits to the same simulator, providing a common execution environment for the comparison. The evaluation covers both hybrid and pure quantum appli-cations: VQE represents a hybrid quantum-classical application, whereas QFT and Deutsch–Jozsa are pure quantum applications. All three applications were evaluated using 5, 10, 15, and 20 qubits.

## 4.2 Validation Experiment 

The validation experiment examines the end-to-end execution of OpenMQPI on a real quantum resource. To analyze the runtime behavior of the pipeline, we instrumented the MQSS plugin and the host application to measure the execution time of its main stages. For the validation experiments, we collected the following runtime metrics: 

• Total Execution (TE): End-to-end execution time of the application. 

• Lowering (TL): Time required to lower the QPIR circuit to OpenQASM and prepare it for offloading. 

• Submit and Wait (TSW): Time between submitting the quantum circuit and receiving the execution results. 

• Serialization (TS): Time required to process the returned measurement results into the format expected by the appli-cation. 

• Constant Time (TC): Remaining execution overhead not included in lowering, quantum job submission, or energy estimation. 

• Energy Estimation (TEE): Time required to estimate the energy from the measurement results. This metric applies only to VQE.

## 5 Related Works 

Several quantum software frameworks and interfaces have been developed to support the programming and execution of quantum applications across different quantum computing environments. These frameworks and interfaces provide different abstractions for representing quantum programs, transforming quantum circuits, and interacting with quantum computing resources. One of these frameworks is Qiskit, an open-source software de-velopment kit for developing and executing quantum applications. It provides a circuit-based programming model in which quantum computations are represented as circuits composed of quantum and classical operations [13]. Qiskit includes a transpiler that transforms and optimizes quantum circuits according to the instruction set and constraints of a target quantum device. While Qiskit performs these transformations within its circuit transpilation infrastructure, OpenMQPI represents and optimizes quantum operations directly within MLIR using the QPIR dialect. Qiskit also supports execution on both quantum simulators and physical quantum hardware, as well as hybrid quantum-classical workflows that combine quantum circuit execution with classical computation [35]. Its modular de-sign also allows the framework to be extended with new circuit synthesis and optimization methods and to target different quantum hardware backends. Another framework is CUDA Quantum, a programming model and software platform designed for heterogeneous quantum-classical computing [18]. CUDA Quantum integrates quantum resources into HPC and GPU-accelerated workflows, providing interoperabil-ity with classical parallel programming models. In HPC environments, the Quantum Programming Interface (QPI) [16] offers a C-based runtime interface for integrating quan-tum programs into HPC applications. Similarly, CQ [6] provides a C-like API, programming model, and specification for developing hybrid applications. Munich Quantum Software Stack (MQSS) is a modular and open-source software ecosystem designed to support hybrid quantum-classical applications and integrate quantum computing resources into HPC environments [7, 14]. Its architecture decouples high-level programming interfaces from the underlying quantum hardware while supporting compilation, resource management, and schedul-ing within the same software stack. Similarly, Quantum Resource Management Interface (QRMI) [2] provides a vendor-agnostic library to access, control, and monitor the quantum resources. It is designed to hide the vendor-specific implementation details by providing interfaces for HPC environ-ments. Mittal et al. propose OpenMP-Q, which extends the OpenMP target model to support the offloading of quantum tasks from C/C++ applications to quantum devices [30]. It introduces a circuit clause to the OpenMP target directive, which passes a user-defined quan-tum circuit to the OpenMP runtime for offloading. At runtime, OpenMP-Q uses a POSIX pipe-based bridge to connect the C/C++ OpenMP application to Python-based quantum software for circuit execution. OpenMP-Q also supports reverse offloading for iterative quantum-classical workflows and the parallel execution of quantum tasks across multiple QPUs. Lee et al. propose Quantum Task Offloading with the OpenMP API to introduce an OpenMP-based programming model to inte-grate quantum computing resources into HPC applications [23]. The approach uses the OpenMP target construct to treat quantum Ercüment Kaya, Ekin Devim Şahinkaya, Minh Chung, and Martin Schulz 

devices as offloading targets, following the same host-device ex-ecution model used for conventional accelerators such as GPUs. Within the target region, quantum circuits are constructed via ded-icated function calls to create quantum registers, apply quantum gates, and measure. These quantum operations are then transpiled into OpenQASM and QIR, which can be passed to their quantum resource manager for further optimization and scheduling on a quantum device or simulator. This programming model is closely related to OpenMQPI, as both approaches integrate quantum execu-tion into the OpenMP target-offloading model. However, while this approach represents quantum operations via dedicated function calls that are subsequently transpiled to QASM or QIR, OpenMQPI represents them directly as QPIR operations within MLIR. OpenMQPI differs from these approaches by introducing QPIR as an MLIR-level representation of quantum operations and by preserving this representation through compiler lowering until the circuit is serialized for the OpenMP runtime. 

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work 

Integrating quantum computing resources into HPC applications remains challenging for portability and reusability, as existing quan-tum programming frameworks rely on different programming mod-els, compiler infrastructures, and execution environments. OpenMP is a well-established programming model in HPC that provides a portable and reusable approach to parallel programming and tar-get offloading. Building on this existing infrastructure, this paper presented OpenMQPI, an MLIR-based compilation infrastructure that integrates quantum circuit compilation and execution into the OpenMP target-offloading model. OpenMQPI uses the QPIR dialect to represent quantum circuits directly within MLIR, enabling quantum-specific optimizations be-fore the circuits are lowered for execution. The resulting circuits are integrated into the OpenMP offloading pipeline and executed on quantum resources through libomptarget and MQSS Client interface. The evaluation demonstrated that OpenMQPI success-fully executes VQE, QFT, and Deutsch–Jozsa through the proposed compilation and offloading pipeline on a real quantum computing environment. Under the evaluated simulator-based configuration, OpenMQPI achieves competitive runtime performance compared with Qiskit and CUDA Quantum. Overall, OpenMQPI demonstrates that quantum computing resources can be integrated into the es-tablished OpenMP offloading infrastructure while preserving a compiler-based workflow for quantum circuit representation, opti-mization, and execution. Future work will focus on extending OpenMQPI in several di-rections. First, integrating the OpenMQPI front end more directly with Clang could provide a more unified compilation workflow for quantum-enabled OpenMP applications. Second, the lowering pipeline could be extended to support QIR as an additional represen-tation, enabling interoperability with a broader range of quantum software and execution environments. Finally, integrating a local quantum simulator would allow quantum circuits to be executed di-rectly within the OpenMQPI environment without requiring access to an external quantum resource. These extensions would further strengthen OpenMQPI as a compiler-based approach for integrating quantum computing resources into established HPC programming and offloading environments.
