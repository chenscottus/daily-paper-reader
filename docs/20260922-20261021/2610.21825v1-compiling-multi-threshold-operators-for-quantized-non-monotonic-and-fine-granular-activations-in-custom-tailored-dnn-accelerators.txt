Abstract—Aggressive quantization of neural networks can
expose activation functions as bottlenecks for efficient hard-
ware acceleration. Implementing quantized activations as multi-
threshold operations has been shown to be a promising opti-
mization, allowing for efficient hardware implementation of few-
bit activations. However, current implementations are limited to
monotonically increasing functions, quantization schemes with
one-dimensional (per-channel) granularity and a small set of
predefined activation-quantizer combinations. We formalize the
multi-threshold representation to extend it to non-monotonic
functions and fine-granular parameters, i.e., multi-dimensional
thresholds. For automated conversion of quantized function
compositions into threshold representation, we propose a sys-
tematic approach via algebraic rules integrated into the ONNX-
based compiler infrastructure. We experimentally validate our
solution on a selection of monotonic and non-monotonic functions
by extending the binary search based multi-threshold operator
that is integrated into the FINN framework for end-to-end
synthesis of custom-tailored FPGA accelerators. The cost of
hardware support for non-monotonicity and fine-granularity is
characterized in comparison to an alternative approach utilizing
existing operators via graph-level decomposition. We compare
our method to prior work as well as the approach taken by the
hls4ml framework. As an outlook, we demonstrate the prospects
of using the multi-threshold representation during the training
of a neural network as a form of quantization aware training.
I. INTRODUCTION
Quantization has emerged as the key model compression
technique for optimizing deep neural network inference [1]–
[3]. Custom FPGA-based accelerators particularly benefit from
aggressive low-bit quantization, typically using 8-bit or lower
precisions [3]–[9]. Streaming dataflow architectures exploit-
ing pipelined model parallelism are well suited for such
deployments, enabling efficient implementations of flexible
quantization schemes. FINN [5], [6], [10] provides an end-to-
end compilation flow for models trained and quantized with
PyTorch [11] and Brevitas [12], with optimizations expressed
through ONNX graph transformations [13], [14].
Quantization methods can be categorized by when quan-
tization is applied – post-training quantization (PTQ) or
quantization-aware training (QAT) – and what is quantized,
i.e., weights, activations, or both [1]–[3]. Early work focused
on quantizing weights and multiply-accumulate (MAC) opera-
tions in fully connected, convolutional, and attention layers. As
these reach increasingly low bit widths, optimizing activations
and layer tails, i.e., chains of element-wise operations follow-
ing a layer, such as normalization, activation, and quantization,
becomes critical [15]–[17]. Simultaneously, preserving accu-
racy requires expressive scale factors, such as floating-point
scales, and fine-granular quantization schemes [15], [18]–[22].
At very low bit widths, implementing quantized func-
tions through successive thresholding, also known as multi-
threshold functions, has emerged as an effective optimiza-
tion [5], [6], [15], [23]. In FINN, multi-threshold functions ex-
actly represent quantized layer tail operations, including non-
linearities and floating-point scales, without costly floating-
point inference. Here, exact means that no approximation
beyond the inherent quantization error is introduced, unlike
fixed-point approximations of floating-point values. Enabled
by streamlining [15], [23], scales, biases, and non-linearities
are absorbed into threshold values through algebraic transfor-
mations, reducing layer tail computations to threshold com-
parisons. In streaming dataflow architectures, these functions
are specialized for each layer tail.
The multi-threshold implementation in FINN is limited to
monotonically increasing functions and, in practice, supports
only predefined activation–quantizer combinations, i.e, com-
binations of quantized identity and Relu. However, modern
architectures employ diverse activation functions [24], [25],
including non-monotonic variants such as Silu [26]. Moreover,
FINN restricts quantization granularity to the final axis of
the activation tensor. While SIRA [15] relaxes some of these
constraints, it relies on expensive compile-time simulation of
the layer tail. Consequently, we identify the lack of a system-
atic and lightweight compilation methodology for translating
quantized layer tails into multi-threshold representation and
corresponding hardware as a key barrier to a broader adoption.
To extend the flexibility and applicability of the multi-
threshold representation, we make the following contributions:
1) We formalize the multi-threshold representation, includ-
ing its assumptions, constraints, and principles for effi-
arXiv:2610.21825v1  [cs.AR]  15 Oct 2026


cient hardware acceleration.
2) We extend the representation and hardware architecture
to non-monotonic and fine-granular functions.
3) We introduce an ONNX-based compiler integration con-

4) We present an initial study of directly learning multi-
threshold representations as a form of QAT, aligning
training-time representation with the target hardware.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Sec-
tion II introduces background and formalism of the multi-
threshold representation, Sections III and IV extend its appli-
cability by addressing monotonicity and granularity limitations
through corresponding hardware implementations. Section V
presents our compiler integration for converting functions into
multi-threshold representation. Section VI discusses related
work and contrasts our approach. Section VII explores the
prospects of learning multi-threshold representations during
training. Section VIII concludes the paper.

about a 2.2× difference) hint at automatic logic optimizations

very low bitwidths (i.e., β < 4) and moderate granularities
(i.e., 1-2 channels), the direct hardware implementation might
be preferable, as decomposition overheads become relevant.

In this section, we first qualitatively compare our work to
prior approaches for compiling multi-threshold operators and
then provide a quantitative comparison to hls4ml.
A. Qualitative Comparison
Table I presents the qualitative comparison. Originally [5],
[6], FINN provides a small set of fixed activation-quantizer
patterns which are converted to a direct implementation of the
summation realized in HLS. SIRA [15] proposes an automated
compilation which works out of the box for any operator sup-
ported by ONNX Runtime and thus does not require operator-
specific extensions. Alongside, a more efficient binary search
based implementation of the multi-threshold operator is in-
troduced, which is available as an RTL backend in FINN.
To avoid the high compilation effort of subgraph evaluation
and search, we propose a semi-automatic compilation, where
primitives are implemented manually but are combined auto-

tail to support more complex activation-quantizer patterns. We
extend frontend and backend to support non-monotonic and
arbitrarily fine-granular functions. Furthermore, it is possible
to use our frontend to target the SIRA backend via graph-level

B. Quantitative Comparison
The hls4ml framework [8], [30], [31] employs a lookup
table approach to implement a variety of activation functions.
The input range is uniformly split into sections and function
output values are tabulated to be retrieved at runtime. hls4ml
does not offer operator fusion, such as the streamlining opti-
mization [15], [23] in FINN. Rather, hls4ml implements each
operator in a layer tail as a standalone hardware operator,
which we refer to as composite approach.
To compare our approach to hls4ml as well as FINN ex-
tended by the SIRA [15] RTL backend, we use a setup similar
to Section V, configuring hls4ml to generate a pipelined design
with II = 1 and a reuse factor matching our PE = 4
parallelism. The results are presented in Figure 9. For simpler
activation functions which are supported out of the box,
hls4ml can implement the function with comparatively fewer
resources, but at the cost of higher quantization errors, espe-
cially for few-bit quantization. We attribute this to the uniform
sampling of the input range, whereas the non-uniformity of
the multi-thresholds allows to better allocate the intervals
to steeper regions of the function, which can also be seen
in Figure 7. For more complex function compositions, the
resource costs of the multi-threshold approach grow only
moderately due to operator fusion. By streamlining a model
graph, which delays the de-quantization scale and bias to the
very end of the graph (dark portion of the bars in Figure 9), this
has the potential to implement the entire quantized activation
data-path in just a few hundred LUTs (at, for example, 4-
bit quantization). In contrast, the composite approach taken

VII. LEARNING THRESHOLDS
As an outlook, we explore the prospects of learning quan-
tized activation functions directly in multi-threshold represen-
tation. This offers an option for training aggressively quantized
neural networks from scratch, which avoids having to find
the ideal activation function at the desired bitwidth, and,
by making the quantization thresholds flexible, brings back
some of the representational capacity usually lost due to low-
bitwidth quantization. Thus, we can interpret learning multi-
threshold representations as a form of quantization-aware
training. Learning of activation functions has been proposed
before, usually by expressing activation functions as learnable
polynomials [32], [33], rational functions [34] or splines [35],
whereas we propose a linear combination of step functions.

VIII. CONCLUSION
We have extended the multi-threshold representation of
quantized functions to non-monotonic functions with fine-
granular parameters by extending the binary search based
multi-threshold operator integrated into the FINN framework
and an alternative graph-level decomposition approach. The
proposed solutions have been evaluated in terms of resource
utilization on an FPGA to characterize the cost of non-
monotonicity and fine-granularity. A systematic and light-
weight compilation approach of quantized layer tails to multi-
threshold representation has been proposed to facilitate end-to-
end synthesis of custom-tailored FPGA accelerators. We have
successfully demonstrated this on a selection of monotonic
and non-monotonic functions. Furthermore, we have proposed
directly learning multi-threshold representations as a form of
QAT, which we have successfully demonstrated on a simple
MNIST classifier. Future work will extend function support
by implementing the necessary inverse functions and further
investigate quality and performance implications of the newly
gained options by using them with more demanding neural
network inference workloads.
