Abstract 

Real-time energy forecasting on edge devices represents a major challenge for smart grid op-timization and intelligent buildings. We present LAD-BNet (Lag-Aware Dual-Branch Net-work), an innovative neural architecture optimized for edge inference with Google Coral TPU. Our hybrid approach combines a branch dedicated to explicit exploitation of tem-poral lags with a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) featuring dilated convolutions, enabling simultaneous capture of short and long-term dependencies. Tested on real energy consumption data with 10-minute temporal resolution, LAD-BNet achieves 14.49% MAPE at 1-hour horizon with only 18ms inference time on Edge TPU, representing an 8–12 × accel-eration compared to CPU. The multi-scale architecture enables predictions up to 12 hours with controlled performance degradation. Our model demonstrates a 2.39% improvement over LSTM baselines and 3.04% over pure TCN architectures, while maintaining a 180 MB memory footprint suitable for embedded device constraints. These results pave the way for industrial applications in real-time energy optimization, demand management, and opera-tional planning. 

Keywords: energy forecasting; edge computing; neural networks; temporal convolutional net-works; deep learning; Internet of Things; smart grid; edge AI 1

# 1 Introduction 

1.1 Scientific Context 

The energy transition and massive deployment of renewable energies require precise and reactive energy forecasting systems [13, 14]. Modern smart grids generate massive volumes of high-resolution temporal data, but their centralized processing poses problems of latency, bandwidth, and confidentiality. Edge computing emerges as a promising paradigm, enabling local data processing with minimal latencies and increased autonomy [1]. However, the hardware constraints of edge devices (limited memory, reduced computing power, energy consumption) impose significant architectural compromises [9]. Traditional deep learning models, although performant, are often too costly for real-time inference on embedded hardware. Architectures optimized for edge must reconcile predictive accuracy, computational efficiency, and low memory footprint [10]. 

1.2 State of the Art 

Current approaches to energy forecasting are divided into several categories. Classical statistical methods such as ARIMA and SARIMA [2] offer high interpretability but struggle to capture the complex non-linearities of energy time series. Recurrent neural networks, particularly LSTMs [3] and GRUs, dominate recent literature thanks to their ability to model long temporal depen-dencies [4, 17, 18]. However, their sequential nature limits parallelization and increases inference times. Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) [5] have recently demonstrated competitive per-formance with better computational efficiency. Causal convolutions preserve temporal causality while dilated convolutions exponentially extend the receptive field. Nevertheless, pure TCNs sometimes neglect explicit exploitation of temporal lags, yet fundamental in energy forecasting where autocorrelations are strong [10]. Transformer architectures [24] adapted to time series have also emerged recently, with spe-cialized models like Informer [6] and Autoformer [7] demonstrating competitive performance [25]. N-BEATS [8] offers an interpretable alternative based on basis expansion. Hardware acceleration via TPU and NPU has transformed the edge AI landscape [9]. Google Coral offers remarkable inference performance for quantized TensorFlow Lite models [11, 12], but imposes strict archi-tectural constraints. The design of models fully exploiting these accelerators while maintaining predictive accuracy remains an open challenge [21, 22]. 

1.3 Problem Statement and Contributions 

This research addresses the following question: how to design a neural architecture for real-time energy forecasting that is simultaneously accurate, fast, and compatible with edge device constraints? We identify three major limitations of existing approaches: insufficient exploitation of lags in TCNs, absence of hybrid architectures optimized for edge TPU, and lack of deployable solutions in industrial production. Our main contributions are as follows: • Proposition of LAD-BNet, an innovative dual-branch architecture combining explicit ex-ploitation of lags and dilated temporal convolutions; • Demonstration of significant improvement in predictive performance on real energy con-sumption data; • Validation of computational efficiency on Google Coral TPU with inference times below 20ms; 5LAD-BNet: Energy Forecasting on Edge Devices arXiv:2511.10680v2 

• Provision of a complete production-ready solution including monitoring, alerts, and real-time dashboard.

# 2 Materials and Methods 

2.1 LAD-BNet Architecture 

2.1.1 Design Philosophy 

LAD-BNet is based on the hypothesis that energy time series contain both explicit dependencies (lags) and implicit patterns (recurring motifs) [10]. A dual-branch architecture allows specializing each branch in the extraction of a type of features, then fusing these complementary representa-tions. 

2.1.2 Global Architecture 

The model accepts as input a sequence of 144 time steps (24 hours at 10-minute resolution) with 27 features per step. The architecture breaks down into three main modules, illustrated in Figure 1: 

Branch 1 (Lag Branch): This branch exploits the last 24 time steps (4 hours) via a deep dense network [3]. Temporal lags (kW_lag_6, kW_lag_12, kW_lag_24, kW_lag_72, kW_lag_144) present strong correlations with future consumption [17, 18]. The sequential architecture comprises flattening of the sequence to a vector of dimension 648, followed by two dense layers of dimensions 256 and 128 with batch normalization and dropout 0.1. 

Branch 2 (TCN Branch): This branch processes the complete sequence via temporal convolutions [5]. Two causal convolutions of 64 filters with kernels of size 3 capture local patterns. A dilated convolution of 128 filters with dilation factor 2 extends the receptive field to the entire sequence. Dual pooling (average and max) extracts the most salient features [10]. 

Fusion Module: Representations from both branches are concatenated then projected via dense layers of dimensions 256 and 128 with dropout [4]. The output layer generates 72 predic-tions corresponding to a 12-hour horizon. 

2.1.3 Justification of Architectural Choices 

The dual-branch design is motivated by feature correlation analysis [10]. Lags present an R2

coefficient of determination superior to 0.85 with future consumption, justifying a dedicated branch [17]. Dilated convolutions [5] offer an exponential receptive field: with a kernel of size 3 and dilation 2, the receptive field reaches 7 time steps in a single layer. Uniform dropout at 0.1 is a compromise between regularization and learning capacity [4]. Batch normalization stabilizes training and accelerates convergence. Dual pooling simultaneously captures the average trend and extrema, crucial information for predicting energy peaks [21]. 

2.2 Feature Engineering 

The complete feature engineering pipeline, detailed in Figure 2, transforms raw sensor data into the 27-dimensional feature vector used as model input.

2.2.2 Lag Features and Rolling Statistics 

Temporal lags at multiple horizons (1h, 2h, 4h, 12h, 24h) provide explicit system memory [17, 18]. Rolling statistics (mean, standard deviation, maximum, minimum) over sliding windows (1h, 2h, 4h) characterize volatility and local trends [2]. These aggregated features reduce sensitivity to measurement noise. 

2.2.3 Meteorological Features and Interactions 

Meteorological features (dry bulb temperature DBT, relative humidity RH) strongly influence consumption via air conditioning load [13]. The temperature-humidity interaction feature (DBT 

× RH / 100) captures non-linear effects on thermal comfort and thus on energy demand [14].

2.3 Data Preparation 

2.3.1 Normalization 

All features are normalized via MinMaxScaler in the interval [0,1] [10]. This normalization guar-antees numerical stability and accelerates gradient descent convergence. The scaler parameters (minimum and maximum per feature) are saved for post-inference denormalization. 9LAD-BNet: Energy Forecasting on Edge Devices arXiv:2511.10680v2 

2.3.2 Temporal Windowing 

Data are transformed into sequences via a sliding window [4]. Each training sample comprises 144 input time steps (24h) and 72 output steps (12h). A stride of 1 generates highly correlated sequences, which favors generalization but increases the apparent dataset. 

2.4 Training and Optimization 

2.4.1 Training Configuration 

Training uses the Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.0005, chosen after grid search on [0.0001, 0.001] [4]. The loss function is mean squared error (MSE), adapted for temporal regression. The batch size of 16 balances gradient stability and training speed. Training is performed over 400 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss (patience 50 epochs). 

2.4.2 Regularization 

Besides dropout, we apply batch normalization after each dense and convolutional layer [3]. This double regularization prevents overfitting while accelerating convergence. Weights are initialized via glorot_uniform, optimal for ReLU activations. 

2.4.3 Train/Validation/Test Split 

Data are divided chronologically: 70% training, 15% validation, 15% test [10]. The chronological split preserves temporal distribution and avoids data leakage. Validation guides hyperparameter selection, while test evaluates final generalization. 

2.5 Deployment on Edge TPU 

2.5.1 TensorFlow Lite Conversion 

The trained Keras model is converted to TensorFlow Lite with full integer int8 quantization [11]. This quantification reduces model size by 75% and accelerates TPU inference [12]. The quantification process uses a representative dataset to calibrate quantization scales, minimizing precision loss. 

2.5.2 Edge TPU Compilation 

The TFLite model is compiled for Edge TPU via the edgetpu_compiler [9]. All operations are mapped to TPU except some minor post-processing operations that execute on CPU. The Edge TPU compilation rate reaches 95%, guaranteeing maximum acceleration. 

2.5.3 Deployment Infrastructure 

The production system runs on Raspberry Pi 4B (8GB RAM) equipped with Google Coral USB Accelerator [9, 23]. The complete edge computing deployment architecture is illustrated in Figure 8. The PyCoral runtime manages the TPU interface. A systemd service guarantees auto-matic restart in case of error. The Streamlit dashboard offers real-time visualization accessible via web browser. 10 LAD-BNet: Energy Forecasting on Edge Devices arXiv:2511.10680v2

2.7 Experimental Protocol 

2.7.1 Baselines and Comparisons 

We compare LAD-BNet to three baselines: a bidirectional LSTM (2 layers of 128 units) [3, 4], a pure TCN (V6.0 architecture without lag branch) [5], and a statistical SARIMA approach [2]. Baselines are trained on the same data with hyperparameter optimization. 

2.7.2 Multi-Horizon Validation 

Performance is evaluated at 5 prediction horizons: 1h, 2h, 4h, 8h, and 12h [10]. This multi-horizon evaluation reveals performance degradation with horizon, a fundamental characteristic of any predictive model [19].

# 4 Discussion 

4.1 Results Interpretation 

4.1.1 Superiority of Dual-Branch Architecture 

Results demonstrate LAD-BNet’s superiority over single-branch architectures [5, 10]. This per-formance is explained by the exploitation of two types of temporal dependencies. The Lag Branch captures strong short-term autocorrelations, a dominant phenomenon in energy series where current consumption strongly predicts immediate future consumption [17, 18]. The TCN Branch identifies recurring patterns (daily, weekly cycles) via convolutions that learn adaptive temporal filters [5]. Late fusion of representations allows optimal specialization of each branch [20]. Experiments with early fusion (concatenation before projection) degrade performance by 1.2 MAPE points, confirming the importance of hierarchical feature extraction. 

4.1.2 Efficiency of Dilated Convolutions 

Dilated convolutions [5] extend the receptive field without increasing the number of parameters. With a kernel of size 3 and dilation 2, the effective receptive field covers 7 time steps in a single layer. To capture 144 input steps, only 3–4 dilated layers suffice, compared to 8–10 standard convolutional layers. Dilation also preserves temporal resolution by avoiding aggressive pooling [10]. This property is crucial for energy forecasting where high-frequency variations (consumption peaks) contain important predictive information [15, 16]. 

4.1.3 Quantization Impact 

Int8 quantification [11] reduces model size from ∼980 KB (float32) to 271.6 KB (int8) without significant performance degradation. MAPE increases by only 0.15% after quantification, a negligible loss compared to the 8–12 × inference speed gain [9, 12]. This robustness is explained by prior feature normalization in [0,1] and the bounded nature of predictions. 

4.2 Positioning Relative to Prior Work 

4.2.1 Literature Comparison 

The 14.49% MAPE at 1h compares favorably to recent literature on energy forecasting [13, 14]. Zhang et al. [21] report 16.2% MAPE with LSTM on similar data. Li et al. [22] achieve 15.8% with Transformer architectures but with 200ms inference time on GPU. Our approach offers a better accuracy-latency compromise suitable for edge computing [1, 23]. Specific work on edge AI for energy is rare [9]. Kumar et al. [23] deploy a quantized LSTM on Raspberry Pi with 17.5% MAPE and 80ms latency without TPU. Coral acceleration represents a significant advance for real-time applications. 

4.2.2 Approach Originality 

The main contribution resides in the dual-branch architecture optimized for Edge TPU [9]. To our knowledge, no prior work explicitly combines lags and TCN [5] in a unified architecture for energy edge computing. The design guided by hardware constraints (int8 quantization [11, 12], TPU-compatible operations) differentiates our approach from non-deployable academic models [21]. 18 LAD-BNet: Energy Forecasting on Edge Devices arXiv:2511.10680v2

4.4 Study Limitations 

4.4.1 Generalization to Different Building Types 

Experiments focus on data from a university tertiary building [15, 16]. Generalization to other typologies (residential, industrial, commercial) requires validation. Consumption patterns vary significantly between sectors, and adapted retraining may be necessary. However, our transfer learning tests suggest the architecture transposes with limited retraining [21]. 

4.4.2 Dependence on Weather Forecasts 

The model uses past weather measurements but not future weather forecasts [13]. Integration of weather forecasts would probably improve accuracy at long horizons ( >4h). This limitation stems from local unavailability of weather forecasts, but API integration is conceivable. 19 LAD-BNet: Energy Forecasting on Edge Devices arXiv:2511.10680v2 

4.4.3 Limited Forecast Horizon 

LAD-BNet predicts up to 12h, insufficient for certain applications (day-ahead market planning) [14]. Extension to 24–48h significantly degrades performance (MAPE > 20%) [19]. A multi-model approach with frequent readjustment could mitigate this limitation. 

4.4.4 Hardware Cost 

The Coral USB Accelerator (€70–80) and Raspberry Pi 4B 8GB (€80–90) represent an initial investment of approximately €150–170 per node [9, 23]. For massive deployments, this cost may be prohibitive. A cost-benefit analysis integrating energy savings is necessary to justify the investment. 

4.5 Research Perspectives 

4.5.1 Attention-Based Architecture 

Integration of attention mechanisms [24] (self-attention, cross-attention) could improve long-term dependency modeling [22]. Specialized Transformers for time series [6, 7, 25] demonstrate remarkable performance but their computational cost remains high. A hybrid TCN-Attention architecture optimized for edge constitutes a promising path. 

4.5.2 Multi-Task Learning 

Extension to multi-task learning (joint prediction of electrical, thermal consumption, and renew-able production) could exploit correlations between tasks [21]. A shared encoder with specialized prediction heads would reduce total parameters while potentially improving each task. 

4.5.3 Adaptive Quantization 

Current quantification (uniform int8) is sub-optimal [11]. Certain critical layers would benefit from higher precision (int16) while others tolerate int4 [12]. Mixed quantification guided by sensitivity analysis would optimize the accuracy-speed compromise. 

4.5.4 Federated Learning 

For multi-site deployments, federated learning would enable continuous model improvement with-out data centralization [21]. Each edge node would train locally then share aggregated gradients. This approach preserves confidentiality while capitalizing on site diversity. 

4.5.5 Probabilistic Forecasting 

Current predictions are point forecasts. Extension to probabilistic forecasts (confidence inter-vals, quantiles) would provide crucial uncertainty information for decision-making under risky conditions [19, 26]. Bayesian architectures or model ensembles are conceivable.
