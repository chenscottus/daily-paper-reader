## Abstract 

Modern edge Systems-on-Chip (SoCs) integrate heterogeneous processing units such as CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs, each with distinct performance and energy characteristics. Deploying AI inference workloads on such platforms under real-time latency and energy con-straints requires jointly mapping workloads to processing units (PUs) and configuring each PU (e.g., by selecting the number of active cores and operating frequency). This joint space grows combinatorially, making exhaustive search for the optimal deployment strategy com-putationally infeasible. Most prior work on design space exploration (DSE) has applied black-box optimization (BBO) methods such as evo-lutionary search to navigate such vast design spaces. An evaluation (simulation or execution on real hardware) is used to obtain metrics, such as latency and energy, to guide the search. Recently, Large Lan-guage Model (LLM)-guided approaches for DSE have gained interest but continue to use sparse feedback similar to BBO approaches. We observe that sparse feedback limits the efficiency of LLM-driven DSE since (i) it does not foster insight into the nature of the design space or the specific reasons that lead a given design choice performing the way it does, and (ii) it does not leverage the full reasoning abilities of LLMs, which are capable of taking richer information from the evaluation of each design point to make higher-quality decisions. Based on these insights, we present TraceDSE, an agentic DSE flow that performs joint workload mapping and PU configuration se-lection for AI inference workloads on heterogeneous SoCs. TraceDSE consists of an iterative proposer–critic loop driven by rich feedback in the form of system execution traces. The LLM proposer agent generates candidate mappings and PU configurations for hardware evaluation. The LLM critic agent, equipped with programmatic trace-analysis tools, analyzes execution traces to identify bottle-necks and suggest targeted refinements. This feedback loop enables deeper insights into each design point, higher-quality decisions, and eventually a more effective search of the design space. Across four AI inference workloads (models of varying complexity and an application pipeline composed of multiple models) deployed on an Intel Meteor Lake SoC, TraceDSE consistently outperforms two state-of-the-art BBO tools. ## 1 Introduction 

AI models are being widely deployed on edge hardware such as mobile phones, wearables, smart glasses, and AR/VR headsets, pow-ering on-device applications across vision (face recognition, gesture and gaze tracking, and computational photography), speech (tran-scription and translation), and natural language processing (personal assistants) [3]. These devices typically contain heterogeneous SoCs that integrate multiple PUs—CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs. Efficiently deploying models onto such SoCs requires jointly navigating two design spaces: the workload mapping space and the PU configuration space . (1) Workload mapping space: A neural network workload is commonly represented as a computational graph, where each node corresponds to a computational kernel, such as a convolution, fully-connected layer or attention computation. Mapping such a graph onto a heterogeneous SoC requires selecting which PU each node executes on. Each PU could offer a different latency–energy trade-off for the same node. For example, a node that upsamples a 48 × 48 feature map to 192 × 192 and applies a 1 × 1 convolution (192 → 1000 channels, ∼7 GFLOPs) when executed on an Intel Meteor Lake SoC takes 46.7 ms and 1.40 J on the GPU, 51.3 ms and 1.04 J on the NPU, and 51.5 ms and 3.81 J on the CPU—the GPU is 10% faster but costs 26% more energy than the NPU, while the CPU matches the NPU’s latency at 3.7 × the energy cost. Beyond these node-level tradeoffs, the overall goal is to utilize the collection of PUs to achieve the desired overall latency and energy goals for the full application.

For exam-ple, on an Intel Meteor Lake SoC, the CPU offers a choice between Performance cores (P-cores) and Efficiency cores (E-cores) with a configurable active core count. The GPU and NPU expose operating frequency control, and the NPU additionally exposes a tile count (number of active compute tiles). The cross-product of choices in these two spaces yields a vast and complex design space. For example, deploying HRNet [18], a pose-estimation model with 98 nodes, across 3 PUs on an Intel Meteor Lake SoC—each PU offering 2 configurations—yields a design space with 3 98 × 23 ≈ 4.6 × 10 47 

candidates. Each candidate requires 30–60 seconds per hardware evaluation, making exhaustive search infeasible. Navigating this design space to identify candidates that achieve the best latency–energy trade-offs requires an efficient search strat-egy. Prior iterative DSE methods follow a common paradigm (Fig-ure 1(a)): an optimizer proposes a candidate (or a population of candidates), an evaluator returns aggregate metrics (e.g., end-to-end latency and energy), and the optimizer uses this feedback to propose the next candidate(s). Black-box optimizers (BBOs) such as genetic algorithms and Bayesian optimization operate within this loop, re-lying solely on aggregate metrics to guide the search [10, 17]. These methods lack the domain knowledge to avoid inferior regions of the design space, leading to slow convergence. Prior work has addressed this by embedding hand-crafted domain-specific heuristics into these optimizers [2, 4, 17], but such heuristics require manual engineering effort for each new platform or workload. More recently, LLM-guided approaches have automated this step, leveraging knowledge embed-ded in pre-trained LLMs to guide the search [6, 11–14, 20]. All these methods still rely on aggregate metrics, limiting search efficiency. Evaluating a candidate on hardware has the potential to yield far more insight than aggregate metrics like latency and energy. An execution trace can provide information about the execution time and energy of each node, cross-PU data transfer times, PU utilization, and more. Figure 1(c) illustrates this on a block from the Inception DNN. The execution trace reveals that the critical-path nodes are mapped to the CPU—making it the bottleneck—and that nodes conv_2 and conv_3 are queued on the GPU while the NPU remains idle. These insights directly suggest two design choices: remapping queued nodes onto idle PUs to shorten the critical path and increasing the active CPU core count to accelerate the critical-path nodes. These decisions cannot be easily derived from aggregate metrics alone. Deriving actionable insights from a raw execution trace requires analyzing per-node execution behavior and reasoning about which mapping and configuration changes would improve performance. LLMs, with their ability to interpret structured representations— execution traces for performance analysis, or workload graphs for mapping decisions—are well-suited to automate this within the DSE loop. These two reasoning tasks are distinct: execution analysis demands detailed per-node traces that grow across iterations, while candidate generation requires a macro view of the design space. This motivates decomposing the DSE loop into two specialized agents: one that analyzes execution traces and recommends improvements, and one that generates new candidates. The key contributions of this work are as follows: 

• We present TraceDSE, an agentic DSE flow for joint mapping and PU configuration selection. 

• We propose an iterative proposer–critic loop in which the proposer generates candidates and the critic analyzes their execution traces and recommends targeted refinements to the proposer. To balance refinement with exploration, a search state maintains the Pareto front from the design points explored thus far and exposes under-evaluated regions of the design space to the proposer. 

• We equip the critic with programmatic trace-analysis tools that provide insight into how individual mapping and configuration choices affect latency and energy, enabling the proposer to act on richer, structured feedback rather than on aggregate metrics alone. 

• We evaluate TraceDSE on four AI inference workloads (models of varying complexity and an application pipeline composed of multiple models) executed on an Intel Meteor Lake SoC. TraceDSE consistently outperforms two state-of-the-art BBO tools. Specif-ically, TraceDSE improves Pareto frontier hypervolume by up to 35% over NSGA-II evolutionary search and by up to 68% over Bayesian optimization, while requiring ∼6–9 × fewer hardware evaluations. 

## 2 Related Work 

Existing DSE methods employ different search strategies within an optimizer–evaluator loop. ArchGym [10] provides a unified framework for comparing search algorithms, including evolutionary algorithms, Bayesian optimization, reinforcement learning, and ant colony optimization, across architectural design spaces. MOHaM [4] uses a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm to jointly search chiplet configurations, chiplet placement, and layer-to-chiplet as-signments for multi-DNN deployment. FARSI [2] augments simulated annealing with architectural heuristics that leverage system-level estimates of compute and memory bottlenecks to guide explo-ration within the SoC design space. Spotlight [17] uses Bayesian optimization for accelerator HW/SW co-design by replacing raw design parameters with derived features, such as PE utilization and estimated DRAM traffic, in the surrogate model. Recent work integrates LLMs into the iterative DSE loop. gem5 Co-Pilot [6] uses an LLM to retrieve and reason over prior simula-tion results to guide L2 cache design. ## 3 TraceDSE Methodology 

In this section, we formulate the joint mapping and configuration problem (Section 3.1), present the TraceDSE framework (Section 3.2), and describe each component in detail (Sections 3.3-3.6).

## 3.2 Framework Overview 

TraceDSE is a closed-loop DSE framework consisting of two LLM agents—a proposer and a critic—connected through an iterative evaluate-analyze-refine loop (Figure 2). Before the loop begins, one-time node profiling measures per-node latency and energy on each PU. In each iteration, the proposer generates candidate design points, which are scheduled and evaluated on the target platform, producing aggregate metrics and an execution trace. The critic analyzes this trace using programmatic tools (Table 1) and recommends targeted mapping and configuration changes to the proposer. A search state records evaluated candidates and maintains the Pareto front and exploration summary, which the proposer uses alongside the critic’s feedback to generate candidates in the next iteration. The loop termi-nates when the relative improvement in Pareto-front hypervolume remains below a specified threshold (0.5% in our experiments) over a specified number of consecutive iterations (five in our experiments). 

## 3.3 Node Profiling 

Before the DSE loop begins, node profiling produces per-node latency and energy measurements on each PU, which we refer to as 

profiling priors . TraceDSE obtains these by running the workload on the target platform once per PU, mapping all nodes to that PU under maximum resource settings. The profiling priors serve two roles: they warm-start the proposer for generating informed initial candidates, and they enable the critic to estimate the end-to-end latency impact of a proposed remapping by substituting a node’s profiled latency on the proposed PU without additional hardware evaluation. 

## 3.4 Proposer Agent 

The proposer generates a batch of candidate design points in each iteration. Each candidate consists of a mapping, a PU configuration, and a reasoning statement explaining its design choices. To generate these candidates, the proposer must determine a mapping of nodes to PUs and a configuration for each PU. (Section 3.3). The workload DAG lets the proposer exploit concur-rency by distributing independent nodes across PUs while respecting ordering constraints. The profiling priors let the proposer avoid clearly inefficient PU choices when constructing initial mappings. In subsequent iterations, the proposer receives three additional inputs to guide its search. The Pareto front captures the best latency– energy trade-offs discovered at any point in the search, guiding the proposer toward regions in the objective space where new candidates would most improve hypervolume. The exploration sum-mary records which mapping and configuration regions have been evaluated, so the proposer avoids revisiting them. The critique from the prior iteration provides per-candidate feedback identifying which design choices drove improvements in the metrics, along with recommended changes to the mapping and PU configuration, which the proposer uses to inform its next batch of candidates. 

## 3.5 Scheduler & Evaluator 

Given a candidate design point, the scheduler assigns nodes to PUs according to the candidate’s mapping and schedules them using a DAG scheduler, Heterogeneous Earliest Finish Time (HEFT) [19], respecting data dependencies in the workload DAG. The evaluator executes the scheduled workload on the target platform and pro-duces the end-to-end latency, energy, per-PU energy breakdown, and an execution trace—a per-node record of the assigned PU, start and end timestamps, compute time, and inter-PU data transfer delays. All evaluation results are stored in the search state. 

## 3.6 Critic Agent 

The critic analyzes each candidate’s execution trace and produces a critique consisting of three parts: per-candidate feedback identifying which design choices drove the measured metrics, so the proposer knows what to preserve and what to change; observations synthe-sized across current and prior iterations that capture recurring patterns—for example, that a particular node-to-PU mapping consis-tently underperforms; and recommended changes to each candidate’s mapping and configuration, informing the proposer’s next batch of candidates. To distill the execution trace into actionable feedback, the critic uses six programmatic tools (Table 1) that it autonomously selects and invokes—for example, first extracting the critical path to iden-tify bottleneck nodes, then inspecting those nodes to determine which alternative PU would reduce their latency. Using the pro-filing priors (Section 3.3), the critic can also estimate the latency impact of a proposed remapping, screening out changes unlikely to improve performance—though these estimates do not capture runtime contention that arises only during hardware execution.

## 5.1 Pareto Front Quality and Evaluation Efficiency 

TraceDSE achieves the highest hypervolume on all four benchmarks while requiring 5.6–9.0 × fewer evaluations (Table 2). At iso-latency, TraceDSE achieves 20–32% lower energy than the strongest baseline on ImageBind and Speech-based VQA (Figure 3, top). At iso-energy, TraceDSE achieves 7–18% lower latency on ImageBind, HRNet, and Speech-based VQA. On three of four benchmarks, TraceDSE discovers low-latency Pareto-front extremes unreached by any baseline—extending 7–14% beyond the fastest baseline—by exploit-ing the critic’s visibility into idle PU intervals and critical-path bottlenecks. TraceDSE’s advantage is larger when the workload offers more opportunities for concurrent execution across PUs. On HRNet, which has repetitive fork–join parallelism, TraceDSE is only marginally better than NSGA-II (1.1% hypervolume gap). On ImageBind and Speech-based VQA, where independent encoder chains can be dis-tributed across PUs, the gap widens to 25–29%. The convergence plots (Figure 3, bottom) further illustrate the sample efficiency of trace-driven feedback: TraceDSE reaches near-final hypervolume within 10–30 evaluations and terminates with a higher hypervolume than any baseline achieves after its full 12-hour budget. Of TraceDSE’s total wall-clock time, hardware evaluation accounts for 60–65%, critic LLM inference for 25–35%, and proposer LLM inference for 4–13%; evaluation on the target SoC remains the dominant cost.

## 5.3 Robustness to LLM Choice and Stochasticity 

To evaluate whether TraceDSE’s performance is sensitive to LLM sampling nondeterminism or the choice of LLM, we vary both independently. Across five independent runs of TraceDSE on Im-ageBind (Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, temperature 1.0), hypervolume ranges from 0.744 to 0.764 ( 𝜎 = 0.009), with 708K ± 88K input and 57K ± 14K output tokens at $4.46 ± 0.65 per run, indicating stable performance across runs. Repeating the five-run protocol on the remaining three benchmarks yields run-to-run variation of the same order. Table 4 reports TraceDSE results on ImageBind using four different LLMs. All four achieve hypervolume between 0.749 and 0.791—a spread comparable to the run-to-run variance of a single LLM—despite up to 5.3 × differences in cost and 1.9 × in wall-clock time. This indicates that TraceDSE’s performance is driven by the proposer–critic architecture and trace-analysis tools rather than by the capability of any individual LLM. 

## 6 Conclusion 

Deploying AI inference workloads on heterogeneous edge SoCs requires jointly optimizing workload mapping and PU configuration across a combinatorially large design space. However, conventional DSE methods treat the hardware as a black box, relying solely on aggregate performance metrics without exploiting the rich structured information available in execution traces. To address this, we present TraceDSE, an agentic DSE flow that analyzes execution traces with programmatic tools to convert each hardware evaluation into actionable feedback for guiding the search. On four single-model and multi-model neural network inference workloads deployed on an Intel Meteor Lake edge SoC, TraceDSE consistently identifies higher-quality candidates and exceeds the Pareto hypervolume of NSGA-II by up to 35% and Bayesian optimization by up to 68%, while requiring ∼6–9 × fewer hardware evaluations.

## A Appendix: Case Study — Proposer–Critic Reasoning on HRNet 

We trace the proposer–critic loop across 12 iterations on HRNet to show how execution-trace feedback drives iterative improvement. HRNet’s 98-node DAG ends in the four terminal nodes hrnet_94 –

hrnet_97 (Figure 4(a)): mutually independent sinks that carry the largest per-node latencies in the workload (41–70 ms each). Their placement therefore largely determines end-to-end latency. Italicized text is quoted verbatim from the run. 

Iterations 0–1. Before the loop, one-time profiling measured each node’s latency and energy on every PU at maximum resources; these priors warm-start the proposer and give the critic a contention-free reference for interpreting traces. In Iteration 1 the proposer first distributed the 98 nodes across all three PUs and reached 283 ms—28% better than the all-GPU seed—placing hrnet_94 on the CPU, which the priors rank fastest for it in isolation (40.2 ms). The trace told a different story: hrnet_94 took 64.2 ms because the configuration enabled only half the CPU cores (“ . . . CPU has only 8 of 16 cores active, causing hrnet_94 to run slower than expected ”), so the critic recommended the GPU (a predicted 1.54 × speedup). The proposer began testing that placement immediately—once in Iteration 2 and three times in Iteration 3—but spent Iterations 2–5 mainly on the 94 non-terminal nodes, and the terminal assignment did not settle; the front grew to span 281–352 ms / 8.2–9.8 J. The GPU placement only became decisive in Iteration 6, once it was paired with the balanced split. 

Iteration 6. The proposer then assigned two terminal nodes to the GPU and two to the NPU, reasoning that spreading independent work across PUs avoids serializing it; the candidate reached 287 ms / 8.3 J, improving both objectives (a new Pareto point). From the trace the critic identified the next bottleneck: hrnet_96 at 57 ms on the NPU versus a predicted 46.6 ms on the GPU (1.22 ×). 

Iterations 7–8. In Iteration 7 the proposer moved hrnet_94 back to the CPU with all 16 cores active, probing whether CPU placement could reduce GPU energy; every candidate was dominated (307– 353 ms).
